Heart Health: Frequently Asked Questions

Here are answers to some of the most common questions patients bring to the office. Every person is different, so use these as a starting point for conversation at your visit — they are not a substitute for your doctor's advice.

Cholesterol and Statins

Do I really need a statin if I feel fine? Very often yes. Statins work primarily to stabilize plaque — not just to lower a number — which reduces the chance of plaque rupturing and causing a heart attack. Feeling fine doesn't mean you're not at risk. This is why prevention starts before symptoms. (See our cholesterol page.)

I heard statins hurt your liver. Serious liver problems from statins are very rare and routine monitoring is quick. For most people the heart benefit far outweighs the risk. Muscle aches can occur; if you have them, tell us — there are often solutions.

Blood Pressure

Is 135/85 considered high? In most cases, that's in the range where we want to lower it, especially if you also have other risk factors. The definition of "high" and a treatment threshold depend on your overall risk, age, and health.

Why is my reading different at home vs. the office? Some people run higher in a medical setting ("white-coat effect") or lower at home. Home readings taken correctly are very useful — that's why we ask you to bring a log. (See our hypertension page.)

Symptoms — What Needs Attention

My heart sometimes skips a beat or flutters. Is this serious? Occasional palpitations are common and often harmless, but they deserve attention if they're frequent, fast, last a long time, or come with dizziness, chest discomfort, or shortness of breath. A quick evaluation or a short wearable monitor can sort it out. (See our atrial fibrillation page.)

Should I worry about swollen ankles? New or worsening swelling in the feet, ankles, or legs, especially with shortness of breath or weight gain, can signal fluid retention — sometimes from heart failure — and should be checked. (See our heart failure page.)

Chest Pain and Heart Attack Fears

When is chest pain an emergency? Chest pressure or discomfort that lasts more than a few minutes, is severe, or comes at rest — especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain in the arm, back, jaw, or neck — calls for 911. Do not wait and do not drive yourself. (See our heart attack page.)

How is chest discomfort different from heartburn? Heart-related discomfort often spreads to the arm, jaw, or back, can come with exertion, and feels like pressure. Heartburn usually sits in the upper belly or lower chest and may improve after eating. Either can feel similar — when in doubt, get it checked.

Medications

I take a blood thinner. Is that safe? Blood thinners (anticoagulants) reduce clotting — which is exactly why they protect against stroke in atrial fibrillation and against stent clots. They raise bleeding risk a little, and it's a balance. Tell all your providers and the pharmacy that you're on one, and never stop it without talking to us first. (See our atrial fibrillation page.)

Why do I take an aspirin? In people who've had a heart attack, a stent, or certain conditions, daily low-dose aspirin prevents dangerous clots. It's not right for everyone, so we choose together based on your individual risk.

Lifestyle

Is exercise safe for me? For most people, yes — and it's strongly recommended. If you have heart disease, have had a procedure, or aren't sure, ask us about a safe plan and about cardiac rehabilitation. Start gently and build up. (See our heart-healthy living page.)

Can I travel or fly after a stent or heart attack? Usually yes, after a short recovery period, but it depends on your situation. Check with us before making plans, and carry your medication list and cardiac ID if you have a device.

Tests and Procedures

What's the difference between a stress test and a heart catheterization? A stress test checks how blood flows to your heart during exercise and helps decide if further evaluation is needed. Cardiac catheterization looks directly inside the heart arteries with dye and is the most detailed test, often used when a blockage is suspected. (Stress test and catheterization pages.)

How do I prepare for a test? It varies — some need fasting, some need you to hold certain medications. We give you clear instructions for each test before you arrive. (See our procedures section.)

Key Takeaways

  • Prevention matters even when you feel fine — that's the whole point of statins and blood pressure control.
  • Palpitations, swelling, and chest symptoms have clear "should be checked" signs — when in doubt, ask.
  • Never stop a heart medication without talking to us first.
  • Ask us anything at your visit; these FAQs are just a starting point.
