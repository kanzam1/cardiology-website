Heart-Healthy Living

The same lifestyle choices that prevent heart disease also treat it — and they are your most powerful tool for protecting your heart at every stage. These aren't rigid rules; they're practical, sustainable habits that fit into real life. Small, consistent changes add up.

Eat for Your Heart

A heart-healthy eating pattern — such as the DASH or Mediterranean style diet — emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, beans, nuts, fish, and lean proteins; limits salt, added sugar, saturated fat, and heavily processed foods; and uses healthy fats like olive oil. You don't have to be perfect — aim for most of the time.

Watch the salt. Excess sodium raises blood pressure. Most people benefit from aiming below a teaspoon-and-a-half of sodium a day (about 2,300 mg, often less for people with high blood pressure). Most sodium comes from packaged and restaurant food, not the salt shaker — so reading labels pays off.

(See our hypertension and cholesterol pages for more.)

Move Your Body

Aim for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity (like brisk walking) — even 10-minute chunks count — plus some strength and balance activity. If you can't manage that yet, any increase from where you are now helps. If you have heart disease or have had a procedure, talk to us first about a safe plan — and ask about cardiac rehabilitation, a supervised way to build fitness safely.

Reach a Healthy Weight

Carrying extra weight — especially around the abdomen — raises blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes risk. Losing even 5–10% of your body weight can meaningfully improve blood pressure and blood sugar. Focus on sustainable habits rather than quick fixes.

Don't Smoke

Smoking damages blood vessels, raises blood pressure and clotting risk, and is one of the strongest risk factors for heart attack and stroke. Quitting is the single most powerful change you can make for your heart — and the benefits start quickly. Ask us about quit plans, nicotine-replacement options, and resources that raise your chances; quitting is hard, but you don't have to do it alone. (See our stroke page for how smoking drives vascular disease.)

Manage Stress and Sleep

Ongoing stress and poor sleep are real cardiovascular risks. Practical help includes regular activity, mindfulness or breathing exercises, staying connected to people, and making sleep a priority (aim for 7–9 hours). If you feel persistently anxious or down after a heart event, that's common — tell us, because treatment and support can genuinely help your heart health too.

Limit Alcohol

Heavy and binge drinking raise blood pressure and the risk of irregular heart rhythms, including atrial fibrillation. If you drink, moderation is key — and cutting back helps almost everyone. (See our atrial fibrillation page for the alcohol connection.)

Know Your Numbers

Ask us what your numbers should be for you and track them at visits:

Key numbers to know (targets vary by person)
NumberWhat it is
Blood pressureUsual goal is below 130/80 for most people.
LDL cholesterolTarget depends on your risk; often below 100 or lower for high-risk patients.
Hemoglobin A1cAverage blood sugar; lower is better, especially with diabetes.
Body mass index (BMI)A healthy range is roughly 18.5–24.9.
Ejection fractionA measure of heart pumping function (for those with heart failure).

Key Takeaways

  • Eat mostly plants, whole grains, fish, and healthy fats; limit salt and processed foods.
  • Aim for 150 minutes of activity a week — any increase helps.
  • Quitting smoking is the single most powerful change you can make.
  • Manage stress, sleep 7–9 hours, keep alcohol light, and know your numbers.
