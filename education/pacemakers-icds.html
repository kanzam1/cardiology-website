Pacemakers and Defibrillators (ICDs)

A pacemaker and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) are small battery-powered devices placed under the skin in the chest to help manage heart rhythms. They are different tools: a pacemaker keeps the heart beating at a normal rate, and an ICD is ready to correct a dangerously fast rhythm. This page explains what each one does and why one might be recommended for you.

What a Pacemaker Does

A pacemaker monitors your heartbeat and sends a small electrical impulse when your heart is beating too slowly, helping it maintain a steady, appropriate rate. Some pacemakers also help the upper and lower chambers contract in the right sequence (biventricular pacing, or cardiac resynchronization therapy, in certain heart-failure patients).

What an ICD Does

An ICD continuously watches your heart rhythm. If it detects a dangerously fast rhythm that could cause sudden cardiac arrest, it delivers a corrective electrical shock (or painless pacing in some cases) to restore a normal beat. It does not make your heart beat and does not keep a slow heartbeat going — that's the pacemaker's job. Some devices, called CRT-D or combination devices, act as both pacemaker and ICD.

Why You Might Need One

Reasons to consider a pacemaker:

  • A heartbeat that is too slow (bradycardia) causing symptoms like fatigue, dizziness, or fainting.
  • A––V block or heart block, where electrical signals pause between the upper and lower chambers.
  • Sick sinus syndrome.
  • Heart failure that may improve with resynchronization pacing.

Reasons to consider an ICD:

  • A previous cardiac arrest or dangerous ventricular rhythm.
  • Severe heart muscle weakness (reduced ejection fraction) with increased risk of sudden rhythm problems.
  • Certain inherited conditions such as long QT syndrome, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or Brugada syndrome.

How They Are Placed

Devices are usually implanted by a heart-rhythm specialist (electrophysiologist) in a cardiac catheterization lab with sedation, not open-heart surgery. A small incision is made below the collarbone, a pocket is formed under the skin, one or more thin leads are guided into the heart through a vein, and the device is connected and tested. The procedure typically takes one to a few hours, and most people go home the next day. There are also “leadless” pacemakers (a tiny device placed directly inside the heart) and subcutaneous ICDs placed under the skin for select patients.

Recovery and Living With a Device

  • First several days: keep the incision clean and dry; avoid lifting the arm on the device side above shoulder height and avoid heavy lifting for a few weeks to let the leads settle.
  • Pain: some soreness is normal and improves quickly.
  • Device checks: you'll have a check soon after placement, then regularly — often by a remote monitor at home plus in-person visits.
  • Battery: devices last many years; battery status is checked at each visit and the device is replaced when needed.
  • ID card: you'll get a wallet ID card; carry it and show it to any healthcare provider, and at security checkpoints (see below).

Daily Life and Safety

  • Airport security: you can walk through the metal detector, but show your device ID card and let screeners know. Avoid holding the wand directly over the device area, and ask for a pat-down if needed.
  • Medical equipment: tell your doctors, dentist, and imaging staff you have a device before any MRI, radiation, or certain procedures.
  • Household: everyday phones and appliances are fine. Keep electronics you hold close to your chest (like earphones in a pocket) away from the device.
  • Strong magnetic fields such as large industrial magnets or certain security systems are generally avoided — we'll give you a personalized list.

Most people return to normal activities, work, travel, and exercise after recovery. Having a device should help you live more fully — not restrict you.

Key Takeaways

  • A pacemaker treats a heartbeat that is too slow; an ICD treats a heartbeat that is dangerously fast.
  • They are placed through a small incision, usually with a short hospital stay and quick recovery.
  • Device checks are quick and often remote; battery replacement is routine.
  • Carry your device ID card and tell providers about it before MRI or certain procedures.
